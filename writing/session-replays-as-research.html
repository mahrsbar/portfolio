What session replays can't tell you

June 2026

Interviews are slow and expensive. Analytics are fast and shallow. Session replays sit between the two, and for a B2B admin tool with a few thousand sessions a quarter they are the best research instrument I have. This is how I run a replay study, and the three ways replays have lied to me.

How I run one

  1. Segment before you watch. Split sessions by who the user is. For me: internal staff acting on behalf of customers, and external customer admins. Same screens, different goals. Averaging them hides both stories.
  2. Baseline first. For each segment, list the most common actions in order. You can't call something friction until you know what normal looks like.
  3. Hunt for loops. Repeated clicks on one control. Tab-switching after a search. Opening and closing the same picker. Cancelling mid-modal. Loops are what confusion looks like in a replay.
  4. Pair with the console. A routing warning or a cancelled request next to a blank screen turns "it felt slow" into a ticket.
  5. Ask the replay tool in plain language. LogRocket's Ask Galileo let me pull "sessions with a Create Users click from non-company emails in the last thirty days" without writing a filter. Use it to test a hunch fast, then verify by watching.
  6. Cross-reference support channels. Six months of "can someone…" messages tell you what people gave up on before any replay started.
  7. Write findings as friction, evidence, consequence. Not "search is confusing". Rather: search only queries the active tab; users see zero results and switch tabs; false negatives become support tickets.

Where replays lie

They show the people who showed up

A replay is a sample of users who opened the product. The admin who stopped trying months ago and now emails support is invisible. My support-channel review found request types with zero replay activity at all: admin handovers, domain approvals. Those were self-service failures so complete that nobody attempted them in the UI anymore.

They show what, never why

A user clicks "show all activities" three times. Confused by a toggle? Impatient with latency? Checking that it worked? The replay can't say, and if you decide, you're guessing with a confident face. This is where shadowing earns its place. A week beside the technical-support team gave me the "why" for a dozen patterns I'd only ever seen as clicks.

Frequency is not severity

The most common pattern in my study was harmless: read-only browsing of a users list. A rare one, a cryptic duplicate-record error with no link to the existing record, was generating a disproportionate share of tickets because every occurrence was a dead end. Rank by frequency and you fix the wrong thing.

What they're genuinely great at

  • Falsifying assumptions. Leadership assumed self-service wasn't happening. Replays showed external admins provisioning users at roughly twice the rate of internal staff. The goal changed from "create self-service" to "stop it failing".
  • Making the case visible. Thirty seconds of a real person hitting a dead end does what a slide never will.
  • Checking a fix landed. After the redesign ships, the same loop-hunt tells you whether the friction went away.

A note on ethics

Replays are recordings of people working. Aggregate before sharing, never screenshot identifiable data into a document, mask inputs at the source, and only watch what the question requires. The goal is understanding the interface, not the individual.
