Case 03Licensing platform

Redesigning a 26-page sidebar as five areas, and a licence application as six guided steps

  • 26 pages → 5 areasSplit by whose work it is, so applicants see only their own
  • 4 tick boxes → 6 stepsEach one guided, with a review that lists what's missing
The problem
A 26-page sidebar mixed the company's pages with each person's. The application gave people nowhere to start: they generated the PDF first, ticked off four steps themselves, and did the paper parts outside the product.
What I did
I redesigned both flows. The sidebar became five areas split by whose work each page is, and each application became six steps, paper tasks included, that end in a review of what's missing.
My role
Front-end developer on a small product team. I ran the research and designed both flows, from the first wireframes to the spec the engineers built from.

Who was applying

Often seasonal staff, assigned an application by a licensing manager and given about two weeks to finish it. Many were nervous about typing their most sensitive information into a portal. I audited the product page by page and sat in on a session with a customer's licensing team, and two things kept getting in people's way: the sidebar, and the application itself.

1. The sidebar

The old sidebar put the company's licensing work and one person's own file in the same column, with related pages spread across groups. I cut or merged what didn't need its own page and split the rest by whose work it is, as a rail of five areas with a panel for each. An applicant now sees only Profiles, and a manager filling in someone's application always has that person's name above it.

The same three things, looked for in the old sidebar and the new one

Choose what to find, and both sidebars highlight where it lives. Both work too: open the old groups, switch areas, or click the old person switcher.

2. The application

The old page opened on the last PDF you generated, with four steps under it that people ticked off themselves. Nothing checked those boxes, and nothing said where to begin or what was left. I rebuilt it as a page of your applications with six steps inside each one, ending in a review that names what's missing.

What each old tick box let through, and what the new review says instead

The same unfinished application in both. This one works: fix a gap and its old tick doesn't change, because the old boxes were ticked by hand.

Where every application now starts, and the six steps inside it

This one works too. Start with Continue on the first application. Everything at full strength responds, and the faded parts of the product sit outside this demo.

How I worked, and what I found

The application flow didn't reach final screens until its prototype had been checked against what a customer's licensing team told me.

What the customer's licensing team said

What they saidWhat it became
Tell me what I missed.→A review step where each warning links straight to its fix.
We want progress bars to see that completion is near.→A count on every step. People speed up when they can see the end, the goal-gradient effect.
People just let their profiles sit.→A Submit step that marks the application done and tells the manager.
Every finding, and what it became

The audit also turned up no sense of how long an application would take, a "request application" button people read as an email, and tasks with no next step.

What I got wrong, and what held up

  • I put the details before the documents. An engineer found forms that ask why a document is missing, an answer that lives in the details, so the two had to be done together. The team merged those steps, and I should have mapped what each field depended on before fixing the order.
  • I left review to the applicant. The customer session had said licensing teams check an application before the notary, and I only gave the manager a heads-up at the end. The shipped version adds a reviewer who can send it back for corrections.
  • I designed an overview page long before the team could build it. By then the product had moved on and it never shipped, though the product manager passed its spec to the next designer as an example of how to set out states for engineers.

The structure held up. The new sidebar, the Applications page and the steps inside each application all shipped, with the six steps grouped into four and the paper tasks still in their own places.

The lesson

When part of a task happens away from the screen, give it a step anyway, with what to do and a place to record that it's done.

The applicant can then see what's left, and their manager can see it's done without asking.

The outcome

An applicant with an assigned application now opens it from a page of their own, sees its status and due date, and is told what's missing before anything is generated. A manager filling in someone else's application can always see whose it is.
