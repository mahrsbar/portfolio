Licensing platform · Product design + front end

Eleven steps down to six

On a licensing-compliance platform, applying for a licence was an eleven-step guide page that ended with printing, notarising, mailing and creating your own licence record. I redesigned it as six steps with visible progress, and gave the managers who assign those applications one place to track them.

Role
Front-end developer on a small team, designing and building the product

The problem

Licensing in regulated industries means long applications, notarised documents, fingerprints and renewals. The people filling them in were often seasonal staff, assigned a form by a manager or left to complete it on their own. The old flow was one long guide page, and my notes from the time sum up what went wrong: no validation, no sense of what was complete, and a platform that was "too open", where nothing was required.

  • No visible end. Sections could be done in any order, with nothing showing what was left.
  • An honour system. Documents were confirmed with a single tick box: "Yes, I have attached all required documents."
  • No submit button. Submission happened outside the product, so people weren't sure when they were finished.
  • Instructions in three places: a cover letter on the PDF, a welcome note and some accordions.

And much of the process isn't software at all: wet-ink signatures, notaries, fingerprints taken at an appointment and mailed in, regulators with their own portals, and renewals that work differently in every jurisdiction. The redesign had to guide people through the paper parts too.

Psych note

The goal-gradient effect

People speed up as a goal gets closer, if they can see how close it is. The step counts, the section ring and a review list that counts down to zero all show it.

Hull, 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006
The home page

An overview that knows where you are

There was no landing page that answered "what do I need to do?". I designed one around three moments: a brand-new user, someone with an application in progress, and someone who's been granted a licence. It's built from widgets people can resize, remove and add back, and every empty state says what will appear there.

The model underneath

Licences first, applications second

The old product was organised around applications, but people think in licences. One licence can need several applications, and one application can produce several licences. So submitting now creates the pending licence record automatically, and the licence record itself got a full rethink: a status history instead of one overwritten field, a "renewal in progress" switch that pauses reminders, and an explicit "no expiry date" option that warns you there'll be no notifications.

I planned the work in phases so each one shipped on its own: navigation first, then this application flow, then assignments for managers, an application manager organised by licence, and finally application packages built from each regulator's requirements.

The manager's side

Assigning applications, and tracking them

Most applicants didn't start on their own: a licensing manager assigned them. I designed that hand-off as one step: pick who the application is for, choose the application, and optionally create a task and email the applicants from a template, all in the same dialog. Each assignment then shows up in the task table, where the manager tracks every applicant's progress through the steps above.

Every client tracked that progress differently: one needed a notary date, another a fingerprint status, another linked licences, and each request arrived as a new column. Instead of adding them one by one, I designed a field builder in settings. Default fields can be switched on or off, and clients add their own as text, date, number, status or person.

The lesson

A queue of feature requests is often one missing setting.

What I took from it

  • Show people the end. Visible progress is what gets a long task finished.
  • Order questions by what they unlock. The jurisdiction came first because it decides which questions even appear.
  • Design the offline parts too. Notaries, signatures and fingerprints happen away from the screen, so they need steps, guidance and a place to record them.
  • Write the rule before the screen. The access model and the licence lifecycle were diagrams and sentences before they were UI.
