The problem

Licensing in regulated industries means long applications, notarised documents, fingerprints and renewals, with different people each doing one specific thing at one specific time. Applying meant filling one huge disclosure form, navigated by a sidebar of dozens of sections that scrolled forever, then working out fingerprints, the notary and submission on your own.

The redesign

Six steps, each one you can finish

Over eighteen months I rebuilt the flow around what people actually had to do, in order:

  1. Start: pick the jurisdiction first, and preview the blank form before committing.
  2. Fill in: sections in a rail with their status, and a completion ring.
  3. Documents: reuse files already on record instead of uploading them again.
  4. Fingerprints: a real-world appointment gets its own step and its own help.
  5. Review: every warning has a button that jumps straight to the fix.
  6. Sign and submit: submitting creates the pending licence record automatically, so nothing is typed twice.

Psych note

The goal-gradient effect

People speed up as a goal gets closer, as long as they can see how close it is. Coffee-card customers buy faster as the card fills, and a card with two free stamps beats a shorter card with none.

The completion ring, the section statuses and a warnings list that counts down to zero all lean on the same effect. Without visible progress, people give up on long forms partway through.

Hull, 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006
The task manager

Twelve requests for twelve columns was one request

The task manager had become the centre of the app, and every client wanted something different: a notary date, a fingerprint step, a start-date column, linked licences. Instead of adding each field, I reframed the queue as one problem, a fixed task model versus the many ways clients actually work, and let them define their own fields and statuses.

Columns could be pinned, reordered or hidden; cells edit in place; and the first screen groups what's overdue and due this week, so it answers “what now?”. I built the beta fixes myself, along with the guided tour that introduced the new table, and tracked every tour step so we knew whether people finished it.

The lesson

A queue of feature requests is often one missing setting.

Turning twelve column requests into custom fields stopped the queue growing, and gave every client the table they'd been asking for.

What I took from it

  • Show people the end. Visible progress is what gets a long task finished.
  • Order questions by what they unlock. The jurisdiction came first because it decides which questions even appear.
  • Every new feature needs an introduction. The tour and banners were as much a part of shipping as the code.
