Screen-time apps gamify the habit they claim to fix. So I built one that doesn't.

Every screen-time app I tried ran on streaks, points and penalties: the same reward loops as the apps they block. LoafMode is a calm iPhone app for scrolling less, where every feature comes from behaviour research.

  • Live on the App Store ↗︎Version 1.1, through Apple's review
  • Built solo with Claude CodeResearch, design, iOS engineering, launch
  • Zero streaks or pointsEvery feature backed by a study

The problem

I wanted an app to help me scroll less, and couldn't find one I liked. They all worked the same way: streaks to protect, points to earn, a pet that gets sad when you slip. That's the same variable-reward loop that makes social apps hard to put down, now pointed at your screen time. And the moment you miss a day, the streak turns into guilt, and guilt turns into deleting the app.

And yes, iPhones already have Screen Time. I used it: it works, but it's cold, there's no one keeping you company, and getting past a limit takes a couple of taps on “Ignore Limit”. I also had reasons of my own: I wanted to take an app all the way to the App Store, and to see how far Claude Code could go on art, not just code.

Step 1

I built the gamified version first

My first version, FocusBlock, did exactly what I'd criticised. Mochi the cat earned XP and treats when you finished a session, there was a day streak, and peeking at a blocked app put Mochi in timeout. It worked, and it felt wrong. Here's the same moment, opening a blocked app, in both versions:

I was using guilt to keep people coming back. So before writing more code, I went to the research on what actually changes phone habits, and rebuilt the app around it.

Step 2

Every feature comes from the research

The rule for LoafMode: nothing nice gets taken away on a bad day. No points, no streaks, no penalties. Instead, each feature comes from a finding about what actually changes phone habits.

  1. 01

    A limit you set once

    Most phone use starts in idle moments nobody planned, and deciding in advance works: if-then plans have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. So the daily limit is the main protection, and it needs nothing from you each day.

    Tran et al., 2019 · Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006

  2. 02

    Starting takes one tap

    Habits form around whatever is easiest in the moment, so friction belongs on the habit you're breaking, not the one you're building. “Focus now” opens one short screen with your last time and apps already set, and the note is optional.

    Wood & Rünger, 2016

  3. 03

    A short wait before more time

    In a field study of 280 people, a short pause before opening an app made them abandon about 1 in 3 attempts, and try to open those apps 37% less often. Anything that gives you more time today waits 10 seconds, with a big “Never mind”.

    Grüning et al., 2023

  4. 04

    A way out, not a lockout

    Strict lockouts work, but they frustrate people. So there are up to three short unpauses a day, each behind the same short wait.

    Kim et al., 2019

  5. 05

    Surprises, never streaks

    Rewards you expect for performing undermine your own motivation; unexpected ones don't. And missing one day doesn't derail a habit. So your companion now and then finds an accessory, nothing shows what's next, and nothing is ever lost.

    Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999 · Lally et al., 2010

  6. 06

    Minutes, not ticking seconds

    Things that start moving grab your attention automatically, and length is easier to judge than angles. So time left shows in whole minutes, never ticking seconds, and progress fills a thin bar rather than a countdown ring.

    Abrams & Christ, 2003 · Cleveland & McGill, 1984

The design rule

An app that helps you use your phone less shouldn't want your attention either.

So LoafMode works offline, has no accounts, earns nothing from time spent in it, and the one deliberately boring screen is the one you see most when it's working.

Step 3

Building it on Apple's Screen Time API

To block apps on iOS you need five pieces that share data through an App Group: the app itself, a monitor that enforces the limit and resets it at midnight even when the app is closed, the block screen, an action that records each reach for a paused app, and the widgets, Lock Screen Live Activity and Dynamic Island.

The Simulator never actually blocks anything, so I added debug launch options that put the app into any state on demand (a limit about to run out, a focus session in progress, the evening palette), and commands that render the widgets, companions and App Store art straight from the code.

The companions were my experiment in AI-made art. Instead of image files, I worked with Claude Code to draw each animal in code, so they stay crisp at every size and can change expression: happy on the hill, calm during a session, surprised on the block screen.

I built it with Claude Code, from the first prototype to the App Store release.

Step 4

Shipping it

Apps that block other apps need Apple's sign-off twice: a Family Controls distribution entitlement for each of the four extensions, then App Review. Review came back asking for more information, so I sent a screen recording of the full flow on a real iPhone and explained each permission, including what the app can't see. LoafMode is now on version 1.1.

What I took from it

  • Build the obvious version, then question it. The gamified version worked, and that's exactly why it was worth throwing away.
  • The research set real numbers. Every timing, wait and word in LoafMode traces back to a finding.
  • Claude Code as a collaborator. It sped up the build and drew the companions with me. The decisions about what the app should and shouldn't do stayed mine.
