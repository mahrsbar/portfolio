From four tabs to one page: designing for fraud analysts

Analysts investigating a suspicious account bounced between a search tool, a map, a link graph and a log viewer, holding the timeline in their heads. I ran the research that made the case for one unified view, designed it, and now build parts of it.

Role
UX research
product design

Status

Problem

Four separate tools, and no single place to see what a user did over time.

What I did

Researched how analysts really work, mapped their journey across products, then designed and iterated the page.

Outcome

One time-anchored view, shipped as the first pillar of a multi-year unification strategy.

What analysts told us

The suite was a set of powerful but siloed tools. Customer feedback said it plainly: there's no complete picture of a user or device, you switch between tabs, some details only live in a log viewer, and "time basis is very important, it's hard to see a timeline with the current tools."

Before proposing anything, I validated that story properly.

Research

  1. Validate workflows with real analysts. Sessions with internal and external fraud analysts, mapping each step, where it hurt, and when they left our product for another tool.
  2. Map the journey across every product. The investigation tool plus the case-management and identity products around it, validated with those teams.
  3. Personas and an ideal flow. Lo-fi mockups of an ideal flow built around four jobs: review the problem, investigate, take action, share findings, deliberately not limited by the current products.
  4. Validate the lo-fi with users and stakeholders before anything was rendered in high fidelity.

The proposal

A unified user page: everything about a user (or device) in one place, so an investigation no longer means opening tabs. The organising element is a timeline: every card is anchored to it, and the time range you pick drives what every card shows.

The proposal became the first pillar of a multi-year "one back office" strategy, with AI tooling and explainability to follow. So the page had to leave room for AI features that didn't exist yet.

From V1 to MVP

  • V1 design for the investigation tool, reviewed with analysts and product.
  • Feedback round. Reworked layout and hierarchy, and made clear which cards the timeline applies to; users found the first version ambiguous.
  • MVP. A final annotated design, built from the component library and handed to engineering.
  • Post-launch fixes driven by observed confusion, including making the timeline's scope visually explicit.

Extending it: device-link analysis

Once the page existed, new signals had a natural home. When a device-integrity feature started finding duplicate devices, I designed how that shows up on the page and the link graph:

  • A toggle to enable the feature and a switch between clustered and unclustered views.
  • Unclustered: an overlay grouping duplicate devices, with a copy-all action on hover.
  • Clustered: duplicate devices collapsed into an expandable node; expanding opens a dismissible popover listing them, again with copy-all.

I built a Figma Make prototype so engineers and stakeholders could try the interactions instead of reading about them. Later ideas were written down too: confidence scores for grandchild nodes, showing why a score is what it is, and export.

Building it

I now implement parts of this page myself, currently its full-text search. Designing something and then building it in the same codebase is the fastest feedback loop I know.

What I learned

  • Find the organising element. Once the timeline was the spine, every other layout question got easier.
  • Validate before you render. Lo-fi flows reviewed by real analysts saved at least one full redesign.
  • Design the room, not just the furniture. Knowing AI features were coming shaped the page's structure before a single one existed.
