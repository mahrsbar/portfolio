What three months of session replays said

Two studies built on data we already had: session replays, support history and a week shadowing the people who answer the tickets. One found why an access console kept generating support requests. The other found why a map said "no results" when there were results. Both ended as ranked fix lists. Press play to watch the pattern behind most of the tickets.

    The method

    For a B2B admin tool with a few thousand sessions a quarter, session replays are the best research instrument I have. The study design was the same both times, and the full method is written up in What session replays can't tell you.

    1. Segment before you watch. Internal staff acting for customers and external admins use the same screens for different reasons. Averaging them hides both stories.
    2. Hunt for loops. Repeated clicks on one control, tab-switching after a search, a modal cancelled halfway. A console warning next to one turns "it felt slow" into a ticket.
    3. Cross-reference support. Six months of "can someone…" messages say what people gave up on before any replay started.
    4. Shadow the people who get the tickets. A week beside the support team gave me the why for a dozen patterns I'd only seen as clicks.
    Study one

    The access console

    The console for managing users, roles and integrations was meant to let customer admins help themselves. Leadership's question was whether that was actually happening and what was in the way.

    The headline surprised people: self-service was already established. External admins were creating users at roughly twice the rate of internal staff, consistently across the whole period. So the goal wasn't to create self-service. It was to stop it failing, and it failed in very specific, repeatable ways.

    FrictionWhat replays showedWhy it became a ticket
    Search that liedThe bar promised "organisation, operator, user" but only matched organisation names, and only in the active tab. People typed an email, saw zero results and switched tabs frantically or gave up.A false "no results" reads as "this user doesn't exist", so admins asked support to find them.
    Nested modalsCreating or assigning a user took two or three stacked modals, each with its own Save. Frequent cancels mid-flow.Nobody was sure whether anything had saved.
    Dead-end errorsCreating a user who already existed (usually pending) returned "this value already exists", with no link to the record.The next step was invisible, so the next step was a ticket.
    Click-shadowsRows, emails, role labels, breadcrumbs and product tags all looked clickable and did nothing.Trained people to stop trying.
    Missing affordanceThe roles dropdown showed "select product first" as static text.Nobody understood which control unlocked it.
    Domain errorsExternal admins blocked by "unauthorised domain", with no explanation of how domains get approved.Without guidance it looked like a bug.

    Support history added the requests the UI had no answer for at all: reassigning an organisation admin who had left, viewing deactivated users, requesting a new email domain. Those were self-service failures so complete that nobody attempted them in the product any more, which is exactly why replays alone would have missed them.

    Psych note

    Learned helplessness

    When actions repeatedly produce nothing, people stop acting, even once the situation changes. Every row and label that looked clickable and did nothing was a small lesson that trying is pointless. By the time admins hit the real dead end, they'd already learned to open a ticket instead.

    Seligman, M. E. P., & Maier, S. F. (1967). Failure to escape traumatic shock. Journal of Experimental Psychology, 74(1), 1–9.

    From findings to a revamp

    I grouped the actions into six themes and reviewed them with product to sort what was achievable, what was out of scope and what needed more investigation: search and discovery, workflow and interaction, error handling and guardrails, performance, support-to-product transitions, and support deflection. Then I prototyped the fix, first in Figma Make for a better organisations page, then in Claude Design when the scope grew into the full revamp. A clickable artefact changed the review conversations: instead of debating a bullet point, the team tried the flow.

    Before: nested modals
    After: one linear flow

    The revamp epic, now in testing, covers the flattened provisioning flow, bulk actions, a duplicate error that links to the existing user, domain-approval guidance, and two new table columns (who invited a pending user, and when). I picked up front-end tickets myself along the way, including ranking direct organisation matches first in search and removing a double search on the users page. What's still open is written down in priority order: row click still does nothing, and the roles affordance is still weak.

    Study two

    The map that said "no results"

    A year after the transaction map got a new version and more filters, a steady stream of complaints arrived: "it says no results, but there's data." I audited the live map with replays and customer feedback, logging every failure with its severity, the version it appeared in, and where the report came from. Twenty-one in all.

    Most of the high-severity ones had a single root: panning and zooming quickly cancelled the in-flight request, the app didn't retry, and the interface rendered the cancellation as "No results found". The data was there. Pins came back only after zooming out, refreshing, or reapplying filters. In roughly one of every six sessions with active map use, the map was lying.

    The quieter failures compounded it: filters that persisted silently across searches, a "show all" button that behaved as a toggle so people clicked it several times, a date picker with no confirmation and a default range of fifteen minutes, and a polygon-drawing mode people entered by accident and couldn't leave.

    Turning the audit into rules

    ObservedRule
    Cancelled request shown as "no results"A cancelled or pending request is a loading state, never an empty state. Empty means the server said empty.
    "No results" vanished too fast to readAn empty state is a signal, not a toast. Make it obvious and keep it on screen.
    Stale filters, stale searchAnything that narrows results must be visible where the results are, with one control that clears everything.
    Default range too smallDefault to twenty-four hours; cap at a year, with an explanation.
    Toggle confusion"Show all" is an override that clears filters, not a mode.
    Accidental polygon modeDelete removes a polygon; fewer apply-then-save steps.

    Each row became a ticket linked from the audit, a note that a fix was already in progress, or an explicit "not a bug, but reconsider". Engineering fixed the request cancellation first, and the rules shaped the interface work that followed.

    The lesson

    Empty and loading are different states.

    A false "no results" is worse than a slow one, because people believe it.

    The outcome

    Study one became the console revamp now in testing: a flattened provisioning flow, bulk actions, a duplicate error that links to the existing user, and domain-approval guidance. Study two's cancelled-request fix shipped first, and its rules shaped the map work that followed. Both ended as ranked lists an engineer could act on without a designer in the room, which is what severity, version and source are for.
